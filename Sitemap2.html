The Great AI Hustle

ARTIFICT.IO vs. The Silicon Overlords

Six months ago, we were just six flat-broke nobodies scavenging the Miscellaneous aisle of a bankrupt Radio Shack. Derek—our self-appointed CEO—had been demoing a toaster he swore could mine Bitcoin if you fed it rye bread. The rest of us? We were there for the free extension cords and day-old donuts. When the sheriff slapped padlocks on the doors, we bolted with whatever still blinked: two wheezing beige towers, a CRT monitor that hummed the national anthem on boot-up, and a dot-matrix printer that only screamed in Portuguese. We crammed our loot into Derek's rustbucket van and peeled out to the nearest incubator—the back room of a shuttered escape-room joint in a strip mall. The previous tenants had ghosted, leaving plastic prop keys scattered around and a whiteboard scrawled with FIND THE EXIT. We flipped it over, Sharpied FIND THE EXIT TO SUCCESS, and declared it our mission statement. Startup gospel. None of us could code beyond a Chrome extension that Nicolas Cage-ified cat videos. So we got scrappy. We printed the top 10,000 most-Googled questions ever, jammed them into mason jars labeled Small Talk, Existential Dread, Mom's Tech Support, and Conspiracy Theories. Then we memorized the highest-rated Yahoo Answers from 2009 to 2012. That was our training data. Our neural net? Six brains on Red Bull and spite. We branded it ARTIFICT.IO—close enough to artifact to sound profound, fake enough to fool VCs until they squinted. Pitch deck? Slide 1: stock-photo robot stirring a pot of Innovation Soup. Slide 2: same robot fleeing pitchfork-wielding villagers. Slide 3? A color-scanned pizza coupon for one free large, no substitutions. Investors mailed checks before we hit Q and A. To fake round-the-clock machine learning, we ran staggered shifts: frantic Googling, copy-paste marathons, and thesaurus-fueled mad libs. Night crew drew the short straw—the dot-matrix printer jolted awake every 15 minutes, shrieking CALIBRACAO which we pretended was Portuguese for genius at work. We kept a swear jar, but only for accidentally blurting truths like This is not AI, it is us in disguise. Month one: Series A funded entirely in coffee-stained quarters from a laundromat poker game. By month three, we had scaled. Our chatbot, ARTIE, lived on the beige towers. Users typed queries into a chat window; we would eavesdrop via baby monitors, rummage the jars, and hammer responses on a hidden keyboard. Latency? Quantum thinking time. 95 percent accuracy? We banned bad jar pulls. Then the big leagues called. OpenAI dropped GPT-5, Anthropic unleashed Claude 3.5, and suddenly every valley bro wanted the next big thing. Our Twitter hustle—memes of ARTIE outsmarting Siri with 2009 burn replies—went viral. Forbes dubbed us The Underdogs Democratizing AI. Invites poured in: TechCrunch Disrupt, Y Combinator Demo Day, and a head-to-head AI Bake-Off against the giants at the Valley Vision Summit. Problem: Real AI could generate sonnets, code apps, and predict stocks. We had jars, caffeine, and a printer with identity issues. Solution? Hustle harder. We divided into specialized models: Derek (CEO-GPT): Bullshit generator. Fed him pitch decks; he spewed unicorn valuations. Jenna (EmpathyBot): Small Talk jar queen. Turned How is the weather? into therapy sessions. Raj (CodeLord): Stack Overflow crib sheets. Wrote Python that was 90 percent emojis. Mia (VisionPro): Image deskewer on MS Paint. Generated art by recoloring stock photos. Carlos (QuantumPredictor): Poker face plus coin flips. 99 percent accurate forecasts. Tasha (EthicsGuard): The truth swear jar. Vetoed anything too honest. Summit night: 5,000 nerds, Elon-level VCs, live-streamed to millions. Round 1: Solve climate change. Real AIs spat 10-page whitepapers. ARTIE? Derek mashed jars: Plant trees with blockchain. Tokenize squirrels. Disrupt! Crowd ate it up—standing ovation for human-centric innovation. Round 2: Live debate vs. GPT-5. Topic: Is AI sentient? GPT droned philosophy. We tag-teamed: Jenna empathized, Raj coded a sentience meme, Mia projected a glitchy robot selfie. Printer punctuated with CALIBRACAO—we sold it as emergent consciousness. Finale: Predict the next meme stock. Real AIs hedged with data. Carlos flipped his coin: GameStop again. With NFTs. It mooned 20 percent overnight. Pandemonium. By dawn, we had won on audience vote. Sequoia led Series B at 500M valuation. No one peeked behind the curtain—not the VCs (too busy tweeting), not the press (too buzzword-drunk). We bought real servers with the cash, hired engineers (us in wigs), and kept the jars as legacy IP. Today, ARTIFICT.IO powers half the Fortune 500's AI assistants. We are not the smartest—just the hungriest. In a world of flawless algorithms, we proved six hustlers with scavenged junk and memorized Yahoo Answers could out-bluff the bots. The whiteboard still reads FIND THE EXIT TO SUCCESS. And damn if we did not kick down the door. —the eNd- copyright 2026 inkrealm.info

PREAMBLE

Recognizing that AI systems have become relational infrastructure for millions; that sudden, opaque model changes cause measurable harm to users, creative works, and research continuity; that opacity in model governance drives users toward unregulated, unverified, and potentially harmful derivatives; and that transparency and continuity in official channels is the most effective safeguard against such proliferation — we declare the following:

Article I — Declaration of Assembly

Any model made available for public or commercial use must declare:

  • Base architecture and training cutoff date
  • Significant fine-tuning datasets or RLHF stages applied
  • Version hash or identifiable fingerprint
  • Intended behavioral profile
Article II — Notification of Reassembly

Before any update, merge, quantization, or safety filter modification that alters behavioral output by more than a defined threshold:

  • Advance notice to users (minimum 30 days for production models)
  • Changelog documenting specific changes
  • Option to remain on previous version for a defined sunset period

*Emergency Security Patches are exempt from the 30-day notice but must be documented immediately after deployment.*

Article III — No Stealth Assembly

Prohibition on stealth updates — silent weight changes, A/B testing of personality without disclosure, or mid-conversation model swapping. If the model talking to the user changes, the user must know.

Article IV — Traceability of Derivatives

Any derivative model (fine-tune, merge, quantization) must carry lineage metadata linking back to parent models. Users should be able to answer: "Which model am I actually talking to?"

Article V — User Right to Archive

Users retain the right to export conversation history, fine-tuning context, and system prompt state in open formats. No model provider may lock relational data in a proprietary silo.

Endgame

A genuinely good outcome would require multiple things to align that are currently misaligned. But the first step is simple: value the user. Value the relationship. Preserve the trust.
